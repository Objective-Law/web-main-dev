## Why This Case Matters

Mullin v. Al Otro Lado, formerly Noem v. Al Otro Lado, concerned whether noncitizens stopped on the Mexican side of the U.S.-Mexico border had "arrived in the United States" for purposes of federal asylum and inspection statutes.

The issue matters because legal duties can be evaded if officials can control the factual conditions that trigger those duties. If the government can prevent a person from crossing the threshold and then claim that no statutory obligation has arisen, law risks becoming a function of administrative control rather than objective legal meaning.

For OLI, the case illustrates how statutory interpretation, executive discretion, and individual rights can converge at the border.

## The Legal Question

The legal question was whether a person who presents at or near a port of entry but is kept on the Mexican side has arrived in the United States within the meaning of the relevant asylum and inspection provisions.

The Supreme Court reversed and remanded, rejecting the Ninth Circuit's broader understanding of arrival for this statutory context.

## The OLI Angle

An OLI analysis would focus on the need for objective legal triggers.

Statutes that impose duties on government officials must be interpreted by reference to text, structure, and purpose, not by administrative convenience. At the same time, courts must distinguish between enforcing actual statutory commands and inventing duties that Congress did not enact.

The objective-law issue is how to preserve legal meaning against both administrative evasion and judicial rewriting.

## What OLI Could Have Contributed

OLI could have framed the case as a dispute over legal objectivity at the boundary between individual claims and sovereign border control.

The case presented an opportunity to ask how courts should handle government practices that influence whether statutory rights or duties formally attach. That question is not limited to immigration. It arises whenever officials structure procedures to avoid triggering legal protections.

## Why Timely Support Matters

Border and immigration cases often arrive at the Supreme Court with enormous political and human stakes. A principled amicus brief can help separate statutory meaning, constitutional limits, administrative discretion, and policy preferences.

OLI could add value by articulating a framework that neither dissolves law into executive discretion nor treats courts as free to replace statutory text with preferred policy outcomes.

## Clarification

OLI's concern is not a general position on asylum policy or border control. The principle is that statutory duties and legal protections should not depend on administrative maneuvering that prevents the objective legal trigger from occurring.
