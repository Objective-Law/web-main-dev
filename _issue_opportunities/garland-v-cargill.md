## Why This Case Matters

Garland v. Cargill concerned whether a bump stock qualifies as a "machinegun" under federal law.

The case was publicly associated with gun policy. For OLI, the deeper issue is objective legality: government may not impose criminal or quasi-criminal liability by expanding statutory terms after the fact.

If Congress wants to ban a device within its constitutional powers, Congress must do so through clear law. Agencies may not transform policy preferences into criminal commands.

## The Legal Question

The formal question was whether the statutory definition of "machinegun" covered bump stocks.

The broader question was whether an agency may reinterpret a criminal statute to reach conduct or devices that Congress did not clearly include.

Fair notice is essential to objective law. Citizens must know in advance what conduct the law forbids before government may punish them.

## The OLI Angle

An OLI analysis would focus on statutory precision, separation of powers, and fair notice.

The legitimacy of criminal law depends on clarity. A person should not have to guess whether an agency will later reinterpret statutory language to convert yesterday's lawful possession into today's punishable offense.

This principle is not limited to firearms. It applies across regulatory and criminal law whenever agencies attempt to enlarge liability through interpretation rather than legislation.

## What OLI Could Have Contributed

OLI could have helped separate the legal principle from the policy controversy.

The issue is not whether bump stocks are good or bad. The issue is who decides, by what legal form, with what notice, and under what constitutional constraints.

Objective law requires that coercive commands be enacted clearly by the proper institution before the government enforces them.

Cases like Cargill often attract issue-specific advocacy. OLI can add a more general account of why legal precision is indispensable whenever government threatens punishment.
